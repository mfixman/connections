	\begin{block}{Computers Can Prove Theorems}
		\large
		Give a \emph{theorem prover} some known facts, called \emph{axioms}, and a claim.
		It searches for a step-by-step proof.

		In 1996, one settled a maths problem that had beaten mathematicians for 60 years \cite{mccune1997robbins}.
	\end{block}

	\vfill

	\begin{block}{Only a Few Axioms Matter}
		\large
		{\diagram{1}{facts}\par}

		\vspace{16pt}

		Read $P(a)$ as ``$a$ is a $P$'', and $\forall x\, (P(x) \to Q(x))$ as ``every $P$ is a $Q$''.
		A prover only learns which axioms mattered after finding the proof.
		Until then, it wastes time on dead ends, like trying to show $S(a)$.
	\end{block}

	\vfill

	\begin{block}{Every Axiom Makes the Search Harder}
		\large
		300 axioms can be combined in more ways than there are atoms in the universe.
		One maths library holds over 50,000 \cite{irving2016deepmath}.
	\end{block}

	\vfill

	\begin{exampleblock}{Our Goal}
		\large
		\vspace{14pt}

		Predict which axioms matter before the search begins, so the prover looks there first.
	\end{exampleblock}
